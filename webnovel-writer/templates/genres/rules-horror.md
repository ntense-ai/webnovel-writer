# Rule-Based Horror Template

> Runtime preset identifier: `rules-horror` (English taxonomy and initialization preset).

> **Core appeal**: Logical deduction + fair clues + intellectual contests. Readers and detective start on equal footing; the truth must be deducible.

## 1. Core Subgenres

### Fair-Play Puzzle Mystery
- **Features**: Pure logical deduction; locked rooms/tricks/alibis.
- **Core payoffs**: Solving puzzles, revealing truth, bringing the culprit to justice.
- **Typical pattern**: Crime → Investigation and evidence → Deduction and revelation.

### Rule-Based Horror
- **Features**: Urban legends, uncanny rules, survival puzzles.
- **Core payoffs**: Discovering rules, exploiting them, escaping alive.
- **Typical pattern**: Encounter a strange tale → Discover rules → Use them to escape.

### Suspense Thriller
- **Features**: Atmosphere, psychological fear, constant reversals.
- **Core payoffs**: Tension, shocking truth, lingering resonance.
- **Typical pattern**: Open a mystery → Peel back layers → Astonishing reversal.

### Social Mystery
- **Features**: Investigates criminal motives and critiques social problems.
- **Core payoffs**: Human depth, social reflection, emotional resonance.
- **Typical pattern**: Behind the crime → Explore human nature → Social critique.

## 2. Ten Commandments of Fair-Play Mystery (Adapted for Web Fiction)

```
1. The criminal must appear early in the story.
2. Supernatural forces cannot explain the solution, though they may mislead.
3. A locked room may have at most one secret passage.
4. Do not use unknown poisons or devices requiring lengthy scientific explanations.
5. A wholly unintroduced mysterious person cannot be the culprit.
6. The detective cannot solve the case through chance or a sixth sense.
7. The detective cannot be the criminal, unless there are two detectives.
8. The detective must share all clues with the reader.
9. The assistant's (Watson figure's) thoughts must be transparent to readers.
10. Twins/doubles must be explicitly established early.
```

## 3. Four Core Pillars

### Pillar 1: Fair Play
```
✅ Correct example:
Chapter 10: The detective finds vase fragments; readers see them too.
Chapter 20: The detective deduces, "The vase was broken from inside."
→ Readers can look back and verify.

❌ Incorrect example:
Chapter 10: The detective finds a clue, but its contents are not described.
Chapter 20: The detective says, "I found the key evidence long ago!"
→ Readers cannot verify it; this is unfair.
```

### Pillar 2: Logic
```
Example reasoning chain:

Known clues:
1. The victim is in a locked room.
2. The windows are locked from inside.
3. The victim holds the key.

Reasoning:
1. Key in the victim's hand → They may have locked the door themselves.
2. Windows locked inside → The killer did not escape through a window.
3. Locked room → The killer may have left before the victim locked the door.
4. Hypothesis: After locking the door, the victim was killed by someone already hiding inside.
5. Verification: Search for hiding places in the room.
```

### Pillar 3: Solvability
```
Clue-distribution principles:
- Key clues appear at least three times in different forms.
- Red herrings must not outnumber genuine clues.
- Give a final hint before the revelation: a challenge to the reader.
```

### Pillar 4: Surprise
```
Reversal levels:
Level 1: Culprit identity.
Level 2: Motive.
Level 3: The case's true nature.
Level 4: Narrator/viewpoint.
```

## 4. Rule-Based Horror Framework

### Rule Design Principles
```
1. Rules must have internal logic.
2. Exceptions require foreshadowing.
3. Escaping through rule exploitation must be plausible.
4. The origin of the rules needs explanation; it may remain mysterious but must be hinted at.
```

### Rule-Based Horror Structure
```
Encounter (20%):
- The protagonist enters an anomalous environment.
- Discovers the first victim/strange phenomenon.
- Receives a rulebook or spoken hints.

Exploration (40%):
- Tests the rules' boundaries.
- Someone breaks a rule and is punished.
- Gradually understands the rule system.

Exploitation (30%):
- Discovers loopholes.
- Plans an escape.
- Encounters surprises during execution.

Conclusion (10%):
- Escape succeeds or fails.
- Final explanation of the rules.
- Lingering implications/open-ended hints.
```

### Example Rules
```markdown
[Elevator Rules]
1. Press only the floor you intend to visit.
2. Do not speak when someone else is in the elevator.
3. If an extra figure appears in the mirror, close your eyes immediately.
4. If the elevator stops on the fourth floor, do not get out.

[Exploiting the Rules]
- Use the no-speaking rule to signal danger to a companion.
- Use the mirror to identify an impostor.
- Use the forbidden fourth floor to lure an enemy.
```

## 5. Outline Structure (Standard: 400,000 Chinese Characters)

### Volume One: The Crime (Chapters 1-30, 15%)
- **Opening hook**: Body/anomaly/disappearance.
- **Introduce key characters**: Detective, suspects, people connected to the victim.
- **Setting**: Enclosed space/time limit/special rules.
- **First investigation**: Gather basic clues.

### Volume Two: Investigation (Chapters 31-100, 35%)
- **Suspect inventory**: Each person's motive, opportunity, and alibi.
- **Accumulating clues**: Physical evidence, testimony, intelligence.
- **Misdirection**: Introduce red herrings.
- **Minor climax**: A second crime/new discovery.

### Volume Three: Deduction (Chapters 101-150, 25%)
- **Integrate clues**: Analyze connections.
- **Test hypotheses**: Elimination/confirmation.
- **Approach truth**: Key clues emerge.
- **Challenge the reader**: Final hint.

### Volume Four: Revelation (Chapters 151-180, 15%)
- **Detective's reasoning**: Complete logical chain.
- **Reveal culprit**: Confirm identity.
- **Reveal motive**: Explain the crime's cause.
- **Explain the trick**: Detailed method.

### Volume Five: Resolution (Chapters 181-200, 10%)
- **Culprit faces judgment**: Legal/moral reckoning.
- **Character outcomes**: Everyone's fate.
- **Aftertaste**: Reflection/hint of a new case.

## 6. Clue Design Techniques

### Three-Layer Clue Method
```
Layer One: Surface clues visible to everyone.
- Time, place, and manner of death.
- Obvious physical evidence.

Layer Two: Hidden clues requiring attention.
- Seemingly casual details.
- Contradictions in dialogue.
- Gaps in the timeline.

Layer Three: Deep clues requiring association/expertise.
- Behavioral and psychological analysis.
- Specialized knowledge.
- Historical/background connections.
```

### Red Herring Design
```
Type 1: False suspect.
- Motive without opportunity.
- Present but possessing an alibi.

Type 2: False evidence.
- Fabricated physical evidence.
- Manipulated witnesses.

Type 3: False deduction.
- The assistant's mistaken inference.
- Deliberate misdirection by the detective.

Type 4: False identity.
- A disguised person.
- Impersonation.
```

## 7. Suspect Management

### Suspect Template
```
Name: ___
Relationship: Connection to the victim.
Motive: Why they would kill.
Opportunity: Whether they had time to commit the crime.
Means: Whether they had the ability to commit it.
Alibi: Present/absent/questionable.
Secret: Hidden information, not necessarily related to the crime.
```

### Recommended Suspect Counts
```
Short work (under 50,000 Chinese characters): 3-5.
Medium work (50,000-200,000): 5-8.
Long work (over 200,000): 8-12.
```

## 9. Classic Trick Categories

### Locked-Room Tricks
```
1. Mechanical locking: timed/remote/automatic.
2. Sneaking in beforehand and waiting.
3. Victim locks the door before being killed.
4. The room is not actually sealed: an illusion.
5. Falsified time of death.
```

### Alibi Tricks
```
1. Falsified time: clocks/recordings.
2. Disguised body double.
3. Twins/lookalikes.
4. Remote killing.
5. Accomplice coordination.
```

### Identity Tricks
```
1. The dead person is not dead: staged death.
2. The apparent culprit is framed.
3. The detective is the culprit.
4. Deceptive narration.
```
